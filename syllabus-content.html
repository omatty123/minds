The course

A dream can undo certainty. A practiced movement can exceed a verbal explanation. Another person’s judgment can enter our experience of ourselves. The works in this course examine mind through philosophical argument, religious cultivation, poetry, music and the telling of a life.

We begin with words and works that do not divide thought, feeling, body and self in the same ways. Each work has its own language, history and purposes. Contemporary neuroscience and AI research enter this conversation as further accounts to examine. No single tradition supplies the definition that all the others must fit.

Students need no previous philosophy or neuroscience. We begin with a scene, passage, sound or image; follow the work’s argument and form; then develop interpretations that can withstand close reading and disagreement.

The word “mind” has a history

English mind comes through Old English gemynd, associated with memory and thought. Its linguistic ancestry connects it with Latin mens and Sanskrit manas. This traces an English word; it does not identify the moment humans discovered mental life. Word-history source.

Everyday English already gives us several uses: “keep in mind,” “change your mind,” “mind the step,” and “I don’t mind.” Remembering, deciding, attending and objecting are related uses, not identical activities.

{{WORDS}}

Shared roots do not guarantee shared meanings. A word’s range does not prove that all its speakers think alike. Translation becomes part of close reading: the passage, period and author determine the relevant sense.

Ancient approaches to mental life

Heart, personhood and judgment

The Greenfield Papyrus offers an Egyptian starting object: the heart has a place in accounts of mind, memory and a person’s continuing existence. Plato’s chariot in Phaedrus 246a–b presents conflict within the soul through an image of driver and horses.

Perspective, practice and liberation

The Zhuangzi and Dhammapada receive sustained study in the slate. Their treatment of mental life connects to ways of acting and living. Their disagreements matter as much as their points of contact.

These are different ancient sources, not stages on a single road toward modern neuroscience. Al-Ghazālī, Descartes, Du Bois and Sōseki belong to later historical settings; the course keeps that chronology visible.

Poetry, music, art and sport throughout the term

Short shared encounters recur alongside the six longer works. They give the class other forms of evidence and expression without adding a new major reading every week.

Poetry and listening

Read Dickinson’s The Brain—is wider than the Sky and I felt a Funeral, in my Brain in full. Return to rhythm, syntax and the difference between a speaker and an author. With Du Bois, keep the Sorrow Songs and musical epigraphs integral to the reading.

Action and shared attention

Study Lygia Clark’s Brazilian participatory work Caminhando through its documentation. Listen to Steve Reich’s Music for 18 Musicians. Bring an athlete’s and a musician’s accounts of trained action into conversation with Cook Ding.

Proposed common events: a translation session; a musician–artist–athlete session on practice; and a conversation about current mind research. Speakers, recordings and dates remain to be arranged. Describing a skill, interpreting an artwork and measuring performance remain distinct kinds of work.

Current research in conversation with the works

The learning bank supports two levels of access: a plain-language account and a manageable source passage for the common course; the complete paper, methods and counterarguments for further study. Research companions are introduced where they sharpen a comparison.

Mark Solms

Feeling and consciousness

The Hidden Spring introduction provides an accessible entry to his account. It can accompany the course’s comparisons of heart, mind and body.

Michael Levin

Cognition across scales

The tissue study opens a comparison between organized biological behavior and experience. Complexity alone does not establish feeling.

Cameron Berg

Claims about AI experience

The self-report study brings the problem of interpreting another mind into a new setting. Read it alongside a competing account and the reported AI correspondence.

None of these accounts is the course’s prescribed answer. Their full technical papers remain available in the bank. Sacks and Le Guin also remain there as alternative sustained works for a later slate.

A possible term sequence

Three meetings per week, with community events taking the place of selected class meetings. This is a pacing model; term dates, holidays and exact reading divisions are not yet set.

From candidate slate to a teachable course

Before adoption, read the selected editions in full, review translations with colleagues, set a sustainable reading load, confirm lawful access and prepare accessible audio and visual alternatives. The bank identifies how deeply each source has been reviewed. The current slate is researched enough to discuss, not yet a completed course reader.

Students should finish able to interpret a work closely, account for a translation choice, compare historically distinct accounts of mind, distinguish an empirical finding from an interpretation, and revise a supported argument. Personal disclosure is not required.
